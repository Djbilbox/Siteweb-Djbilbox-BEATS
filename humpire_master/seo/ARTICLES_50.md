# How to Make a G-Funk Lead Synth That Whines

The G-Funk lead is a monophonic, portamento-heavy sine-to-saw tone that sings over a bouncy bassline. It is simple to build once you know which controls matter.

### Start monophonic with glide
Set the synth to mono and add 60-120 ms of portamento so notes slide into each other. Play legato: overlap each note slightly so the glide triggers instead of retriggering the envelope.

### Pick a bright but smooth waveform
Use a saw or a saw blended with a sine an octave above. Low-pass the filter around 3-5 kHz with low resonance; the whine comes from pitch movement, not from filter squeal.

### Add slow vibrato late
Route a 5-6 Hz LFO to pitch at a very low depth, faded in after the first beat of each note. Mod wheel control makes it expressive without sounding mechanical.

### Keep the melody simple
G-Funk leads repeat a short two- or four-note motif with long held notes and a slide at the end. Leave space for the snare and the bass; two bars of melody is enough.

# G-Funk Bass: How to Write the Bouncing Moog-Style Line

The G-Funk bass is a round, slightly detuned synth bass that follows the kick and leaves room for the lead. Rhythm matters more than sound design.

### Choose a round waveform
Use a sine plus a soft saw or a filtered square. Keep the cutoff low enough that the harmonics do not fight the vocal range.

### Lock to the kick
Place bass notes on and just after each kick hit. Syncopated pickups on the 'and' of beats 2 and 4 give the characteristic bounce.

### Use octave jumps
Alternate the root with its octave on offbeats. A short slide into the octave adds movement without adding notes.

### Control the low end
High-pass at 30 Hz, and avoid stacking sub from the bass and a long kick. If they clash, shorten the kick or sidechain gently.

# West Coast Drum Programming: Snaps, Claps and Swing

West Coast drums are sparse and laid back: a punchy kick, a tight snare or clap, and shuffled hats. The groove, not the sound count, makes the genre.

### Build the backbone
Kick on 1 and a syncopated kick near beat 3, snare or clap on 2 and 4. Keep ghost notes quiet and sparse.

### Swing the hats
Apply 55-62% swing to closed hats and vary velocity by hand. Open hats on the offbeat before the snare add lift.

### Layer snap and clap
Layer a finger snap with a clap and nudge the snap 5-10 ms early so it sounds like a single hit with attack.

### Pick the right one-shots
Choose dry, slightly vintage kicks and snares. A curated one-shot kit saves time compared with digging through folders.

# Vocoder vs Talkbox: Differences and When to Use Each

Both effects make instruments speak, but they work differently and sound different in a mix.

### How a vocoder works
It splits the voice into frequency bands and uses their levels to shape a synthesizer carrier. The result is clean, synthetic and precise.

### How a talkbox works
The original hardware pushes the synth sound into your mouth and records the shaped result. Plugins emulate this with formant filters.

### Which sounds more human
Talkbox keeps more natural vowel color and attack; vocoder sounds cleaner and more robotic.

### Choosing for your genre
G-Funk and funk favor talkbox, electro and synthwave favor vocoder. Many producers use both on different sections.

# Maqam Basics for Producers: Hijaz, Rast and Bayati

Maqam is the system of melodic modes used in Arabic music. A few basics are enough to write convincingly.

### Hijaz
Characterized by a half step, an augmented second, then a half step. It is the most recognizable sound in Oriental fusion beats.

A bright, major-like mode with a neutral third. In 12-tone software the third is often approximated.

### Bayati
A mode with a quarter-tone second. Use pitch bend or microtonal tools for accuracy.
